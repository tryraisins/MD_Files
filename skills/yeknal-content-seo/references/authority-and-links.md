# Authority and Links

A perfect page on a site nobody links to ranks near the bottom. Links and mentions are how a site becomes eligible for competitive results, and they compound only when they are real.

Google's [link spam policy](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) is the boundary: links created primarily to manipulate ranking, including paid links that pass credit and excessive reciprocal exchanges, are violations on both sides. Everything below is earned coverage. If a method requires hiding what it is, it is not in scope.

## Expect loss

Links disappear. Sites close, pages are rewritten, directories are pruned. A large share of what a campaign earns will be gone within a year or two without anyone doing anything wrong.

Price the work against the links that will still exist later, not against the number earned this quarter. A modest set of durable, relevant links beats a large set that evaporates.

## Methods, in order of cost

### Claim mentions that already exist

Someone wrote the brand name and did not link it. Roundups, newsletters, forum threads, podcast notes, "tools we use" pages, conference pages.

This is not a favour request. The page already decided the brand was worth naming. Find them by searching for the brand name and the domain, then asking each page to add the link to the existing mention.

### Replace links pointing at dead sites

Find domains in the niche that lapsed. Pages still linking to them now send readers to an error. Those pages have a broken link and an editorial reason to fix it, and the replacement is a page that covers the same subject.

Only offer the replacement where it genuinely replaces what was there. Do not claim to be something the site is not.

### Ask to be added to lists that already include the category

Filter before writing, or the email is spam:

- the article ranks for a query that matters;
- it was updated recently enough that the author is still maintaining it;
- it already includes at least one competitor, which means the author has accepted that the category belongs in the piece.

Then write a short, specific email: what the list covers well, what is missing, one link, and an explicit invitation to ignore it if it does not fit. Offer a free account or a sample where that is genuinely useful, not as a transaction.

A mention without a link is still a useful outcome. Those roundups are what assistants read when someone asks which tool to use.

### Study who links to competitors

A site linking to three competitors has already decided the category deserves a link. It is a qualified prospect, not a cold one.

Build one table: the linking site, the page, which competitor it links to, and a contact address found on the site. Never invent an email address.

### Publish something citable

A homepage is not citable. A writer needs a specific thing to point at:

- original data, with the method and the sample stated;
- a free tool that solves one narrow problem;
- a reference page that is more complete than the alternatives;
- photographs or footage nobody else has.

Ranked by durability: original data first, then a tool, then a reference page, then an infographic, which is easy to copy and rarely cited for long.

### Participate where the audience already is

Communities, video, and social platforms rank on domains the site does not own. Choose the surfaces where the buyers are, not the ones with the largest audience.

- Answer questions as yourself, with the affiliation in the first line.
- Answer properly, including where the product is the wrong choice.
- Do not post a burst of promotional replies. A steady, small presence survives moderation; a campaign does not.

### Events, press, and bylines

Local press covers events, and those links usually point at the homepage rather than the page that needs to rank. Bylines put a person's name on a domain the engine trusts, but they depend on one editor. When that editor leaves, the links stop. Treat both as supplementary.

## Outreach mechanics

- One email, one or two follow-ups, then stop.
- Small daily volume, sent by a person or a reviewed queue, never an unreviewed blast.
- Read replies and answer them. Most opportunities die while a yes sits unanswered.
- A separate sending domain is common operational practice, but the sender must still identify who they are, honor opt-outs, and comply with the applicable unsolicited-email law.
- Track the outcome per prospect: sent, replied, linked, declined, no response.

Automation is fine for finding prospects, deduplicating, and drafting. A person approves what leaves.

## Partnerships and swaps

Publishing an honest ranking that includes competitors is legitimate. Once that page ranks, other businesses want to be in it. That is editorial demand, and it is the strongest position a small site can reach.

Two rules keep it clean:

1. Would the page include this business if it offered nothing in return? If not, do not include it.
2. Disclose the relationship. Never make inclusion conditional on a reciprocal link.

A handful of swaps between businesses that genuinely belong together is an editorial decision. A ring of sites linking to each other on request is a footprint, and Google's policy names excessive link exchanges.

## Paid links

Read the policy before the sales page.

- A link that passes ranking credit in exchange for payment violates the policy for both buyer and seller.
- A compliant paid link carries a sponsored or nofollow qualifier, which removes the ranking credit that was being sold.
- Anyone selling a "safe dofollow placement" is selling a policy violation with a reassuring name.
- The usual outcome is not a penalty. It is a link that is discounted, so the buyer paid for nothing.

If a user decides to buy anyway, be explicit about what is being bought: traffic and placement, not ranking credit. Buy outside the link economy; a site that sells links to anyone is not a site whose recommendation means anything.

When evaluating a link profile, including your own, these patterns indicate inventory built for sale rather than earned coverage:

- almost all anchors are an exact match for a commercial phrase;
- every link comes from one top-level domain;
- every site runs the same CMS and the same ad slots;
- there are no nofollow links at all, which no real brand achieves;
- the traffic curve is declining, or the traffic is unrelated to the page selling the link;
- the homepage features the same "featured article" layout across unrelated categories;
- the price is low enough that the same placement must have been sold many times.

None of these proves intent on its own. Together they describe a page that exists to sell slots.

## Directories and listing surfaces

A directory entry is a citation surface and a small referral channel before it is a link. Treat it as one.

Qualify each directory before submitting: a real editorial standard that rejects some submissions, evidence of organic traffic, a category the product genuinely belongs to, an indexable page reached from a crawled page, and no footprint of a network built to sell slots. A list that accepts everything is a page nobody reads.

Paying a person to file submissions is ordinary freelance work. Paying for a link that passes ranking credit is the violation described above, on both sides, so a placement advertising "safe dofollow" is that violation renamed. A compliant paid link carries `sponsored` or `nofollow`.

Vendor claims about a domain-rating jump or an acceptance rate stay unverified until the live URLs exist, and a listing that asks for a badge in exchange for a link is a swap. Track each submission by directory, live URL, link status and referral sessions, and record rejections so the reason can be fixed rather than retried.

## AI citation prospecting

Assistants that answer from live search read pages that already rank or are already cited. The work is to be named on those pages.

1. Build the query set from the commercial queries already chosen, in the forms an assistant would search: "best [category] [year]", the same with a country, "[brand] reviews", "alternative to [competitor]", and a site-restricted query against authority domains.
2. Run the queries logged out, on a fixed schedule, and record the query, date, market, engine, and which URLs were cited. A logged-in account is personalised and gives a different answer.
3. Read the sources, not the answer. Across a set of queries the same few listicles, review pages, and threads recur. Those pages are the prospect list.
4. Earn inclusion honestly, with the disclosure and the accuracy rules above.
5. Check whether the site ranks itself first in its own comparison content. Readers and models discount self-ranking, and the claim is usually not true. Rewrite the ranking on the merits; the site can still appear, placed honestly.

Do not assume one engine behaves like another, or that this month's pattern holds. Citation share by surface moves quickly; verify before committing a quarter to one channel. Answers also differ between the public app and an API, so sample the surface the audience actually uses.

A domain occupies only one or two organic positions on a page. If the result page also shows video, community threads, and third-party lists, the site can compete only by also appearing in those layers, or by being the subject of them. Rank opportunities by how many non-website surfaces already occupy the result, not only by demand.

## Measurement

Track:

- referring domains gained and lost, with the page linked and the anchor;
- branded search volume and branded clicks;
- referral sessions and assisted conversions from earned coverage;
- citations observed for the fixed query set, by engine and date.

A third-party authority score is an estimate. The evidence that matters is clicks and conversions on queries that matter to the business.

## Failure modes

- Starting with paid placements because they are available today.
- Buying a placement described as safe dofollow.
- Reciprocal links arranged as a transaction.
- Self-ranking first in the site's own listicle.
- Emailing a list that has never included a competitor.
- Sending from an address that hides who is writing.
- Treating a one-off citation as a durable position.
- Reporting a domain score instead of business outcomes.
