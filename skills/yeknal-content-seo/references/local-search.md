# Local Search

Local visibility is not national SEO with a city name added. When someone searches a service plus a place, the top of the result is usually a map with three business profiles. Those are profile listings, not websites. Position four in that map is effectively page two.

The profile competes on three factors:

- **Proximity.** Distance from the searcher to the address. It cannot be changed.
- **Relevance.** How confidently the engine associates the profile with the exact service. Content and categories fill it.
- **Authority.** How much the web trusts the business. Links and reviews fill it.

Most competitors filled their profile once and stopped. Relevance and authority are the two factors a business controls.

## Measure before changing anything

Use a rank grid: a set of points across the service area, each reporting the profile's position for one query.

1. Record the keyword, the grid size, the date, and the device or location settings.
2. Compute the top-three share: points where the profile appears in positions 1 to 3, divided by all points. Show the division.
3. Describe where the strong area is and which direction is weakest.
4. List the points in positions 4 to 6 separately. These are the cheapest gains.
5. Use only the numbers in the export. Do not estimate a point.

Position 4 is worth zero in this measure. A profile that is fourth everywhere has no map visibility, whatever the average position suggests.

## Profile audit

Three failures appear on almost every neglected profile.

### Categories

The engine allows a primary category and additional categories. Each one opens a different set of searches.

- Aim for a small set of true categories, not all available ones.
- Never let a model choose categories from memory. Invented category names sound as plausible as real ones.
- Get the real list from the platform's own category search, or from what the strongest local competitors use, paste it, and choose only from that list, spelled exactly.
- Reject any category that describes a service the business does not offer.
- If nothing on the list fits, say so. Do not invent one.

### Services

Each service should exist under the category it belongs to. A competitive profile lists many more than the handful the owner entered at setup. Add the services the business actually performs, in the customer's words.

### Completeness

- Description at the platform's full length, written for a person.
- Enough recent photographs to show the work, the team, and the premises.
- Hours, attributes, service area, and contact details accurate and identical to the site.
- Posts on a maintainable schedule, not a burst.
- Booking or quote links that work on a phone.

### Reviews

The text matters as much as the rating. A review that names the service and the place tells the engine more than ten that say the service was good. When asking for a review, ask the customer to mention what was done and where.

Do not invent reviews, customers, or results. Do not offer incentives where the platform prohibits them.

## The page the profile points to

For a single-location business this is usually the homepage. Check:

- title and H1 contain the primary category and the city;
- a map embed of the profile;
- the secondary categories as sections with headings;
- a reviews display that reflects the real profile;
- the address, character for character as on the profile;
- the phone number, character for character;
- service descriptions that match the profile's services.

Most local homepages have the business name as the title. That is free ground.

## The core page set

Mirror the profile on the site:

- one page per category, such as "drainage service [city]";
- one page per service, such as "water heater replacement [city]";
- the homepage as the profile landing page.

Order the set by measured demand, highest first. That is the writing order. Every page links down from inside a paragraph, not only from a menu.

## Page quality for each place

A place page must be about the place:

- the housing and building stock, and the problems that follow from it;
- the routes and response times the team actually covers;
- prices or ranges that apply there;
- photographs and jobs from that area;
- landmarks and neighbourhood names people use.

A page where only the place name changes is the doorway pattern described in [page decisions and briefs](page-decisions-and-briefs.md). If there is nothing specific to say, there should not be a page.

## Decide the next stage with one number

After the first set has been live for a fair period, measure the top-three share again for the primary query, then measure it for the three or four businesses that dominate the map.

- The leader's share is the practical ceiling under current proximity.
- A reasonable target is a fraction of that ceiling, set by the project rather than by a universal rule.

Below the target: the engine does not yet believe the business is the best answer. Build content that answers the questions customers ask, and strengthen the profile.

At or above the target: expand outward to the places in the weak grid areas, with pages about those places.

## Multiple locations

One page per location, with its own staff, hours, photographs, reviews, and directions. Do not clone one page across locations with the name swapped, and do not point every location at one shared page.

## Measurement

- Profile insights: calls, direction requests, website clicks, bookings.
- Rank grid: top-three share, trend over time.
- Site analytics: local landing page sessions and conversions.
- Reviews: count, rating, and recency.

Proximity caps what any profile can achieve. Report the cap alongside the result so a business is not judged against a location it cannot serve.

## Failure modes

- Optimising the website while the profile stays incomplete.
- Categories chosen from a model's memory.
- One service and one category, set three years ago.
- Review requests that produce "great service" with no service and no place.
- Location pages that differ only by name.
- Judging map visibility by average position instead of top-three share.
- Promising a map position without measuring the grid first.
